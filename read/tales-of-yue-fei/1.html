Chapter 1 · Heaven Sends the Red-Whiskered Dragon Down; the Buddha Exiles the Golden-Winged Bird to Earth

The Complete Tale of Yue Fei · by Qian Cai & Jin Feng

For more than three hundred years the history of the Song unrolled, and within it the South and the North warred across the land. At leisure let me comment on the affairs of the two emperors — loyalty and righteousness that move one to grief and to admiration. Loyal and true, they endured frost and dew beneath a blazing sky; the crafty and wicked are autumn gnats in the moonlight. Glory and disgrace rise and fall, all empty fame — yet the dream of the yellow millet will not wake!

A poem says: the wars of the Five Dynasties would never rest, and only after the yellow robe was laid upon his body did a ruler find peace. Who could have known that a Southern lord who fled and entrenched himself, forsaking loyal and good ministers, would leave the myriad people to grieve? Heaven's cycle has its turnings, its rising and its decay. This very poem of mine gives rise to the account of a loyal marshal of the Southern Song who devoted himself with all his heart to serving his country.

Now in the late Tang and Five Dynasties, one morning men served the Liang and by evening the Jin, and the common people suffered. At that time, on Mount Hua in the Western March, there lived a recluse named Chen Tuan, styled Master Xi Yi, an immortal of lofty virtue. One day, riding his mule across the Tianhan Bridge, he raised his head, beheld five-colored auspicious clouds, and suddenly burst into laughter — and fell off his mule. When the crowd asked the reason, the master said, "Good, good! Do not say there is no true lord in the world — two dragons have been born at a single birth." Gentlemen, why did he speak these words? For there was an official household, surnamed Zhao, named Hong Yin, who held the post of Situ; his lady, of the Du family, bore him a son in the Jiama camp, named Kuang Yin — the mortal incarnation of the Thunderbolt Immortal of the Upper Realm — and so red light, rare fragrance, and auspicious clouds attended him. When Kuang Yin grew up he became a hero beyond compare: with one staff and two fists he subdued four hundred army-prefectures and founded the empire of the Great Song, with its capital at Bianliang. Ever since the mutiny at Chenqiao when the yellow robe was laid upon his shoulders, he was called the "Dragon of the Manifest Omen," and he passed the throne to his younger brother Kuang Yi — hence "two dragons born at a single birth." From the founder of the dynasty down to Huizong, the throne passed through eight emperors: Taizu, Taizong, Zhenzong, Renzong, Yingzong, Shenzong, Zhezong, and Huizong.

This Huizong was the mortal incarnation of the Long-Browed Immortal of the Upper Realm. He was excessively fond of gods and immortals and styled himself the "Emperor of the Way." By then the realm had long been at peace — truly: horses set free on the southern hills, swords and spears stored in the armory; the five grains ripened in abundance, and the myriad people lived content. A poem says: Under the sage rule of Yao and Shun the three-fold blessings were celebrated; the satiated sang songs across the land. With wind and rain in season the people rejoiced in their work, and the herded cattle and horses abandoned the weapons of war.

Let idle chatter be set aside. It is said that the Buddha Tathagata of the Great Thunderclap Temple in the Western Paradise of Ultimate Bliss, one day seated upon the nine-grade lotus throne, was attended by the Four Great Bodhisattvas, the Eight Vajras, the Five Hundred Arhats, the three thousand chant-keepers, bhikkhunis and bhikkhus, upasikas and upasakas, and all the protecting saints of the heavens, who together listened to the preaching of the supreme Dharma. Just as flowers rained from heaven and jewels showered in profusion, an astral official — a female bat — who chanced to be seated beneath the lotus throne listening, could not resist letting out an unclean fart. The Buddha, being the Lord of Great Compassion, thought nothing of it. But the guardian spirit seated above the Buddha's head, the Golden-Winged Bright King of the Great Roc, with golden light blazing in his eyes and auspicious radiance upon his back, beheld the filth of the female bat and grew enraged. Spreading his wings he swooped down upon her and struck her head, pecking her to death with a single bite. That one ray of the female bat's spirit shot out of the Thunderclap Temple and flew straight to the Eastern Land to seek a mother and be reborn — in the mortal world as a daughter of the Wang household, who would one day marry Qin Hui and so repay the grudge.
